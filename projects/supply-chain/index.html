Case study · 2026 · Group project

Global supply chain optimisation for a perfume manufacturer

A written account rather than a repository. The parameter tables behind the model are University College Cork teaching material and are not mine to redistribute, so the code stays private and the reasoning goes here.

Asked
How much new capacity does the manufacturer need to build?
Found
Less than they thought. 20,000 of the 35,000 unit monthly shortfall could be covered by capacity already sitting idle, at a net saving of EUR 150 to 250 per unit.

The brief

01 / 08

Groupe Elegance makes perfume through four suppliers, three factories in France, two warehouses and six markets from Paris to Shanghai. Monthly demand is 275,000 units against 240,000 units of factory capacity, so some demand goes unmet whatever the plan. The brief was one optimisation covering sourcing, production, workforce, warehousing and distribution that balanced cost, service, reliability and emissions, and said what to do about the gap.

The data, and why it is not here

02 / 08

Nine parameter tables supplied with the module: supplier prices, capacities and delivery variance; factory capacities, unit costs and emissions; staffing costs and absenteeism; market demand, urgency and penalty rates; warehouse capacity and storage cost; transport cost, time and emissions by mode; distances; product recipes; and the transport network.

They are UCC teaching material, not open data, so neither the tables nor the notebook that reads them are published. What follows is method and result.

Preparation

03 / 08

The tables arrived as nine separate exhibits. I consolidated them into one workbook and loaded that into Python as the model's parameters, so every coefficient in the objective and every constraint bound traced back to a named cell and a change in an assumption was a change in one place.

The recipes were the fiddly part: product formulas in grams and millilitres per unit against supplier capacity in kilograms, reconciled so the material balance constraint would hold without a hidden unit error.

The model

04 / 08

A mixed integer linear programme in PuLP. Seven classes of decision variable: kilograms bought from each supplier, units of each product made at each factory, a binary switch per factory, integer staff per role, inventory per warehouse, units shipped per warehouse, market and mode, and unmet demand per market.

The objective minimises eight cost components: procurement, fixed and variable production, labour, storage, transport, a carbon charge of EUR 0.05 per kg CO2, and unmet demand penalties weighted by market urgency. Constraints cover supplier capacity, factory capacity, material balance from the recipes, workforce net of absenteeism, warehouse capacity and demand with slack.

The binary factory variables are the reason it is a MILP rather than an LP. Without them a plant could be a fraction open and pay a fraction of its fixed cost, which no plant does.

What came out

05 / 08

An optimal cost of EUR 81.4M. Transport was 49 percent of it and unmet demand penalties 46 percent; everything else together was under 5 percent. All three factories run, producing 220,000 units and meeting 80 percent of demand.

The solver serves the high urgency markets in full, New York, Paris, Tokyo and Shanghai, and lets Milan and Dubai absorb the shortfall because their penalties are lowest. It also makes only Product C, the least material-intensive recipe, to get the most units out of constrained inputs.

Cost and capacity

06 / 08

The shadow prices said where the constraint actually was. Grasse and Lyon were at capacity with zero slack. Raw material constraints had full slack, so supply was not the problem. Bordeaux was running at 70,000 of 90,000 units, which left 20,000 units of built capacity doing nothing.

Left as it was, the shortfall costs EUR 37.5M a year in unmet demand penalties. Each unit added at Bordeaux was worth EUR 150 to 250 in penalty avoided, net of production and transport cost. That is the number that turned "how much do we build" into "switch on what you have first".

What was recommended

07 / 08

  • Activate Bordeaux's idle 20,000 units first, with the workforce to run them, and treat new capacity as the second decision rather than the first.
  • Buy from EthanolPro and LuxeCap first. Hold Capella, the costliest and most volatile supplier, to the minimum the product mix needs, and carry a two-month buffer of rare extracts.
  • Route by urgency: air for New York and Tokyo, sea from Marseille for Milan and Dubai.
  • Keep the EUR 0.05 per kg carbon charge as the operating default and stress test hard emission caps quarterly rather than living under one.

Limitations, and my part

08 / 08

The core model is single-period and deterministic: one month, known demand. Teammates extended it with a two-stage stochastic version and a 500 iteration Monte Carlo, which is where the plan gets its range. The optimised plan produces 220,000 units, which is also the combined warehouse capacity, so any expansion case should confirm warehousing does not become the next constraint. And the recommendations were coursework, never implemented.

My part. Group project. I prepared the data, built the MILP, did the cost and capacity analysis and wrote the recommendations. Teammates built the routing layer with Dijkstra in NetworkX, the two-stage stochastic model and the Monte Carlo simulation. The 82 percent cut in modelled CO2 against an all-air baseline, largely from moving non-urgent lanes to sea, is theirs.
