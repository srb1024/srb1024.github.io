Case study · 2025 · Group of five

Customer churn prediction for a telecoms provider

A written account rather than a repository. The dataset belongs to University College Cork and is not mine to redistribute, so the code stays private and the reasoning goes here.

Asked
Which customer segments are we losing?
Found
The demographics the business had been segmenting by barely mattered. Payment delinquency, short tenure and competitor pressure did.

The brief

A telecoms operator was losing 26 percent of its customer base and wanted to know who was leaving. The instinct in the brief was demographic: which age groups, which income tiers, which regions. The job was to build a model that could flag customers at risk early enough for a retention team to do something about it, and to say what was actually driving the risk.

The data, and why it is not here

50,000 customer records and 35 variables covering billing, tenure, service usage, support contact and demographics. The class balance was 74 percent retained to 26 percent churned, which is enough imbalance to matter for how you score a model but not enough to need resampling.

The dataset is a teaching set supplied by UCC. It is not open data, so I have not published it or the notebook that reads it. Everything below is method and result, which is the part that transfers anyway.

What I did

Preparation

The raw categorical fields were inconsistent, the same value recorded under several spellings and cases. Standardising those and one-hot encoding the result gave 114 predictors. I added two behavioural features that the raw data did not have: spend per month of tenure, which separates a long-standing customer on a small plan from a new one on a large plan, and recent engagement against historic activity, which catches a customer whose usage has dropped before their bill does.

Model comparison

Six classifiers were compared on the same stratified 3-fold cross-validation, scored on ROC-AUC rather than accuracy because with a 74 to 26 split a model that predicts "retained" every time is already 74 percent accurate. XGBoost came out ahead at 84 percent accuracy and 86 percent precision on the held-out set. Precision was the metric the business needed, because every flagged customer costs retention effort and a model that cries wolf gets switched off.

Interpretability

A score on its own is not something a retention agent can act on. I applied SHAP for the global picture and LIME for individual customers, so each flag arrives with its top reasons attached. The SHAP summary was the finding: demographic variables such as income tier and gender clustered near zero contribution, while billing and behavioural features carried the model.

What came out

Three drivers dominated. Payment delinquency, whether a customer had missed or delayed payments. Short tenure, with risk concentrated in the first six months. And competitor pressure, customers who had been contacted by or had recently compared rival offers. The demographic segments the business had been organising its retention effort around were not where the risk was.

The useful output was not the model. It was the reordering of what the business thought it already knew about its own customers.

What was recommended

  • Score customers continuously inside the CRM rather than in a quarterly batch, so the flag arrives while there is still time to act.
  • Incentivise auto-pay enrolment, since payment delinquency was the strongest single lever and it is one the business controls.
  • Concentrate retention effort in the first six months of tenure, where risk peaks and where a small intervention has the longest payoff.
  • Give retention agents the LIME explanation alongside the score, so the conversation starts from the customer's actual situation.

Limitations

The data is a single snapshot, so the model finds association rather than cause. A customer who misses payments may already have decided to leave, in which case auto-pay incentives arrive too late for them and only help the next cohort. Testing that needs a time dimension the dataset does not have. The recommendations were also never deployed, so their effect on actual churn is untested. What is defensible is the ranking of drivers and the case for continuous scoring over demographic segmentation.

My part

Group of five. I owned the data preparation and feature engineering, ran the model comparison and did the SHAP and LIME interpretability work. Teammates handled the exploratory analysis and the written report.
