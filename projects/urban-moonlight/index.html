Case study · 2025 · Group project

Urban Moonlight, smart streetlighting for Cork City

A design thinking project rather than an analytics one. The output is a working prototype and a testing framework, so this is an account of how the brief changed and why.

Asked
How do we light Cork's streets better after dark?
Found
Visibility was not the barrier. Fear of falling and of being alone if you did, was what kept elderly and disabled residents indoors. That turned a replacement project into a retrofit.

The brief

01 / 08

Reimagine streetlighting in Cork City. The assumption in the room and in most of the literature, was that the answer was brighter lights. Our job was to find out whether that was actually the problem before anyone designed anything.

Who it was for

02 / 08

We mapped everyone the change touches and sorted them into three rings by how directly they interact with a streetlight. Elderly residents, disabled residents, students, tourists and night walkers sit in the core. Night shift workers and delivery drivers sit in the middle. City councils, professionals and emergency services sit outside: they fund it and govern it but rarely stand under it after dark.

That ordering mattered later, because the people who pay for the scheme and the people who use it want different things from it.

What people actually said

03 / 08

Six personas, then an empathy map for each, splitting what people say and do from what they think and feel. The gap between the two halves is where the finding came from.

Out loud: the pavement is hard to see, the kiosk is too complicated. Underneath: I am afraid of falling, I do not want to look lost or vulnerable, I would rather stay in. The behaviour that followed was avoidance. No evening walks unless someone came along, familiar routes only, daylight for the shops and the GP.

The reframe

04 / 08

Journey mapping made it obvious. Mood starts hopeful, holds while walking familiar ground, drops at the point of using public technology and only recovers on the way home. The low point is a kiosk, not a dark stretch of pavement.

Every persona feared something different. The elderly feared falling, students feared losing contact, tourists feared getting lost and the digitally excluded feared complexity. Underneath all four was the same unmet need: confidence.

How might we create an inclusive, adaptive lighting system that helps pedestrians, especially elderly and mobility-restricted users, feel safer and more confident in Cork City's public spaces?

Ideation and what we threw out

05 / 08

Everyone ran their own Crazy Eights, then we clustered. What survived shared one property: it had to work for people the technology usually overlooks. Accessibility panels with tactile and audio, motion-triggered brightening, charging, community voting.

What we cut is the more useful half. AR projections and ground light projection, because they distract drivers and obstruct paths. Parcel lockers, because a pole should not block a pavement. A short-trip taxi button, because it needed personal data we had no business collecting. Selling environmental data to developers, because it served someone other than the people in the core ring.

The prototype

06 / 08

A retrofit, not a replacement. Existing poles keep their place in the ground and gain a weatherproof touch panel, motion-triggered lighting, a physical Read Aloud button with braille imprinting, USB-C and e-bike charging and a bench for anyone who needs to stop.

The detail that came straight out of the research is the countdown. Every SOS gives ten seconds to cancel, because the research said the fear of looking foolish in public was as real as the fear of falling. A button you cannot un-press is a button nobody presses.

The service behind it

07 / 08

A pole is only the visible part. Service blueprints traced two journeys end to end, an elderly walk and a student commute, across what the user does, what they touch, what happens front of stage, what happens behind it and which systems have to be running.

The analytics brief followed from that. Anticipate and pre-empt: a failing bulb, a newly dark stretch, a storm coming. Fix the hazard before anyone meets it, which is what turns a lighting scheme into a confidence one.

Testing, limitations and my part

08 / 08

The testing framework runs in two phases. Usability sessions with 12 to 15 Cork residents over 50, mixed technical confidence and mixed mobility, think-aloud protocol, with targets of 90 percent task completion and a System Usability Scale score of 70 or better. Then a pilot of three to five working poles for two to four weeks on Patrick Street and Grand Parade, measured on daily active users, how evenly the features get used, issue reports and satisfaction tracked separately by vision, mobility and technical confidence.

None of it has been run. This is coursework, so the prototype was tested for comprehension inside the module and the framework above is a plan rather than a result. The research is real, the reframe is defensibleand the numbers in the framework are targets, not findings.

My part. Group project. I ran ideation, built the working prototype in Figma Make and defined the usability and pilot testing frameworkand contributed to the stakeholder and empathy research behind it.
